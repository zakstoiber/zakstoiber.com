Divisiveness

Interaction splits players. Quiet engine-builders unite them.

Divisiveness compares how spread out a game's ratings are with games of the same rating, weight, popularity and era, and places it among all games at 100+ ratings: top 10% means more spread out than 90% of them. Games built on role playing, acting, player elimination, hidden roles and voting land in the top 19% to 24%. Games built on layering, grid coverage, contracts and worker placement land in the bottom 27% to 33%.

Themes follow the same line. City building, farming and industry are the most agreed-on; collectible components, miniatures and zombies are the most divisive. That fits the Cole Wehrle read: his games sit at a median top 16%, the most divisive of any prolific designer.

Designers

Designers' later games rate higher, but only because they're newer. They don't get heavier.

For the 657 designers with 10+ ranked games, the median rating climbs from 6.36 on a first game to about 6.7 by the 20th. Against the rating model, which accounts for weight, release era, popularity and genre, the line is flat: first games beat expectations by 0.01, and games 21 to 50 by 0.02. By this measure, experience doesn't show up in the ratings.

Weight doesn't move either. A designer's games average about 2.05 on BoardGameGeek's 1–5 scale from the first game to the fiftieth, and a designer's 8th to 10th games come out heavier than their first three for 49% of designers, a coin flip. Individual designers do move, but in both directions, so it cancels out. Dávid Turczi, Daniele Tascini and Uwe Rosenberg went much heavier; Corey Konieczka, Vlaada Chvátil and Thomas Lehmann went much lighter.

Pairings

Some mechanisms are inseparable, some almost never meet, and wargames keep to themselves

Each square compares how many games carry both tags with how many would if tags were handed out at random. The tightest group is the wargame kit: hex grids, simulation, dice rolling and grid movement end up together whether I group the top 20 mechanisms or all 77, and whichever grouping method I use. A few pairs are just as dependable, like hand management with take that and set collection with open drafting. The bigger groupings around them shift with those choices, so the chart orders mechanisms by how they pair but doesn't name families.

The gaps are as sharp as the pairings. Only 4 cooperative games use auctions, where chance would give about 97, and only 9 trick-taking games roll dice, against about 102. Among themes, wargames are an island: 2 are also party games, against about 209 by chance, and almost none are puzzle, children's or animal games.

Unusual combinations neither pay off nor cost anything. Games whose mechanisms rarely go together rate about the same against the rating model as games built from familiar pairs: the five groups' medians sit within 0.02 of each other.

Player ages

Players think the box age is too high for light games and too low for heavy ones

BoardGameGeek users vote on the youngest age a game works for. For 6,418 games with 5+ votes, players set a younger age than the box 47% of the time and an older one only 16%. The gap flips with complexity: for the lightest games players say about a year younger, and for weight 4+ they say more than a year older. Publishers' ages bunch between 8 and 14; players spread them out.

Wishlists

The games people want more than they can get

Across games with 2,000+ ratings released by 2020, BoardGameGeek users wishlist about one copy for every seven they own (median 0.14). These twelve are wishlisted at four to seven times that rate. Half of them were crowdfunded, against a fifth of games overall, and the rest include long out-of-print classics like Roads & Boats, Container and Leaving Earth.

Wishlisted and owned are BoardGameGeek collection counts from the October pull. Crowdfunded means the game carries a BoardGameGeek "Crowdfunding:" family tag. Out of print is my reading of these titles, not a field in the data.

Series

18xx is BoardGameGeek's biggest series, and close to its heaviest

128 ranked games carry the 18xx series tag, 84 of them with 100+ ratings, more than any other series. Their median weight is 3.95, heavier than 98% of games, with a typical play time of four hours. They rate well, but mostly because they're heavy: against the rating model they come out only 0.16 above expected, 40th of 134 series. They are wishlisted about twice as often per copy owned as a typical game, and fewer people own one for each rating, which fits small print runs and a lot of play online and at conventions (my reading, not a field in the data).

The most-rated 18xx games

Across series, the ones that beat expectations most are old wargame lines like Great Campaigns of the American Civil War and La Bataille, the GIPF abstracts and Mayfair's crayon-rail Empire Builder games. Furthest below are look-alike and novelty lines (Monopoly-like and Risk-like games, World's Smallest, LEGO), along with Devir's Small Box Euros and Daniele Tascini's T games.
