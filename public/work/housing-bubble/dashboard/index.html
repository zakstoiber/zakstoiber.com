Why the housing market isn’t a bubble

Sorry, first-time buyers.

All my first-time homebuying friends are waiting for the bubble to burst (no judgment). And since I spent 12 years in mortgage insurance, I’m often the bearer of bad news (for them): The data clearly shows this is not a repeat of the housing crash. 2008 was a credit bubble – easy loans pumped up demand on top of a building boom. This time, prices rest on a shortage of homes and on owners with cheap loans, plenty of equity and a habit of paying on time. Here’s the thing about a crash: it needs forced sellers, lots of people who have to sell right now at any price. In 2008 there were millions. Today there are almost none, and I’ll use the power of charts to explain why throughout the page.

The puzzle

Yes, prices are higher than in 2006… even after inflation.

This is why so many would-be buyers think it’s a bubble, and I don’t blame them. After inflation, US home prices are — above their 2006 peak, and Milwaukee is — above its own. They’re way above the old trend line too. Not ideal! But high prices alone don’t make a bubble. A bubble has to be held up by something that can vanish, like easy credit. A shortage of homes can’t vanish overnight. Prices can still cool as they drift back toward what buyers can afford, but that looks very different from a crash.

                    Why it won’t pop, the recap

                    No forced sellers, no crash

                    Remember the forced sellers? This is where it usually clicks for my friends “waiting for the bubble to burst.” 2008 had a conveyor belt of them: prices dipped, owners went underwater, payments reset, banks foreclosed, and the foreclosures pushed prices down again. Today that conveyor belt is mostly empty. People with equity and a payment they can afford don’t have to sell, so a weak market just gets… quiet. Fewer sales, flat prices, no fire sale.

                            For first-time buyers

                            So what do I do?

                            Asking for all my friends.

                            I’m not your loan officer, but here’s what the data says if you’re trying to buy:

                            • Don’t plan around a crash. Even 2008, the worst housing crash in modern memory, took about — years to bring prices down —.
                            • Watch the payment, not just the price. On a typical home with 5% down, a 1-point drop in mortgage rates cuts principal and interest by about — a month: — at this spring’s — rate, — at —.
                            • You probably don’t need 20% down. Most buyers don’t put that much down. Mortgage insurance or an FHA loan adds to the monthly cost, but it can get you in the door sooner.
                            • Look where the supply is. Builders are sitting on more unsold new homes than usual, so new construction may be where there’s room to negotiate.
                            • In Milwaukee, don’t expect prices to fall first. Building here has lagged the most, and prices are still climbing after inflation, up — since mid-2024.

                            The devil’s advocate

                            How this could still turn into a crash

                            Arguing with myself so you don’t have to.

                            A crash needs forced sellers, so the real question is what could create millions of them again. It would take a shock big enough to break several of the safeguards above at the same time. Here’s how that could happen.

                            • A deep recession with lots of layoffs. Equity and fixed rates protect owners who keep their jobs. They help a lot less if millions lose their jobs at once, burn through their savings and stop paying. In late 2009, US unemployment hit 10%. Today it’s —. A jump that big would test everything on this page.
                            • An AI bust could be the trigger. Counting Amazon and Tesla, tech companies are now — of the value of all US stocks, against — at the dot-com peak in early 2000. Stocks are nearly as expensive compared with earnings as in 1999, and spending on computers, software and data centers is a bigger slice of the economy than in 2000. If that spending stalls, the damage would come through layoffs in tech and construction, and through stock losses that make people spend less. History is mildly reassuring: from 2000 to 2003 the S&P 500 fell —, but mortgage rates fell too and US home prices rose —. The risk is a bust big enough to spread into layoffs everywhere.
                            • The newest buyers have thin cushions. The average owner has plenty of equity, but recent buyers who put 3% to 5% down don’t. About — of US mortgages owe more than 90% of the home’s value, so a 10% price drop would push those owners underwater. That’s a small share, but these are mostly recent buyers, and many of them are the first-time buyers this page is about.
                            • Insurance and property taxes. A fixed-rate mortgage doesn’t fix the rest of the housing bill. Homeowners insurance rose 8.7% faster than inflation from 2018 to 2022, and in the fifth of ZIP codes most exposed to climate damage, people pay 82% more and get dropped by their insurer about 80% more often (US Treasury). Mortgage escrow payments, which cover taxes and insurance, are up about 45% in five years and 70% in Florida since 2019, and in the states where they’re rising fastest, more borrowers are falling behind (Cotality). That’s one way owners with cheap, fixed loans could still become forced sellers.
                            • Places that built a lot. The extra unsold new homes in the warning signs above aren’t spread evenly. Prices are most likely to fall first in the fast-building areas, such as parts of Florida and Texas, and when builders cut prices to clear inventory, nearby owners feel it too.

                                  How this was built

                                  • Sources. Every housing number comes from the agencies’ own files rather than a data portal, mostly multi-tab workbooks, zipped XML and fixed-width text: FHFA’s House Price Index, National Mortgage Database and Monthly Interest Rate Survey; the Federal Reserve’s Financial Accounts (Z.1); the New York Fed’s Household Debt and Credit Report; Census new construction, new home sales, building permits, construction spending and housing vacancies; BLS consumer prices and national and local unemployment; Freddie Mac’s mortgage rate survey; and Zillow. The devil’s advocate adds the Ken French Data Library, Robert Shiller’s stock data, BEA, SEC EDGAR filings and Yahoo Finance prices. Buy now, pay later comes from the Fed’s household survey (SHED) and a CFPB report.
                                  • Timing. Quarterly values average the months in each quarter; balances are end of quarter. “Then” values use the year before the crash (2005 to 2008) or the crisis peak, as the text says. In the forced-seller chain, each “then” is that measure’s worst quarter on record, so the four come from different dates between 2008 and 2013. Q4 2025 BLS and Census survey values cover November and December only, because October 2025 data wasn’t collected during the federal funding lapse.
                                  • Gaps. FHFA doesn’t publish loan rates, loan-to-value or credit scores for metro areas, so Wisconsin stands in for Milwaukee. Underwater and rate data start in 2013, after the worst had passed, so the underwater “then” understates 2008–2011. Credit scores at origination are Equifax Risk Score through 2025; the NY Fed moved to VantageScore 4.0 in 2026, so 2026 isn’t compared.
                                  • Prices. “After inflation” is FHFA’s all-transactions index divided by CPI-U. The long-run trend is a log-linear fit to 1980–1999 (about 0.4% a year), extended to today; a 1% a year trend would still leave prices about 23% above it. The 2008 decline uses the unadjusted index: the highest quarter before 2010, the lowest after it, and the first quarter back at the peak. Price-to-rent divides by BLS rent of primary residence (seasonally adjusted from 1981), with the 1983–1999 average = 100; owners’ equivalent rent gives a similar picture. Milwaukee uses Midwest rent, and its own CPI rent, which ended in 2017, agrees. CPI rent tracks what existing tenants pay, so it lags new-lease rents.
                                  • Payments. The new-buyer payment is principal and interest on Zillow’s typical US home value (seasonally adjusted, so a little below the spring figures in Zillow’s monthly reports) at Freddie Mac’s 30-year rate, divided by average disposable income per household (Fed personal income over Census households). Average income runs above the median, so compare years rather than read the level. The 5%-down case adds mortgage insurance at an assumed 0.5% of the loan a year, a round figure for a strong-credit borrower; real premiums vary with credit score and coverage. The “watch the payment” tip compares the Q2 2026 rate with one point lower, before mortgage insurance, taxes and homeowners insurance, rounded to the nearest $10.
                                  • Down payments. The National Mortgage Database covers all purchase loans from 1998 and counts piggyback second loans. The older Monthly Interest Rate Survey covers conventional first mortgages from 1973 to 2018. The two aren’t spliced.
                                  • Devil’s advocate. Tech is Ken French’s 12-industry “BusEq” group (computers, chips, software, internet services and electronic equipment): number of firms × average firm size from CRSP, shifted to month-end, through July 2026. Amazon and Tesla add month-end split-adjusted price × split-adjusted shares outstanding from each 10-K and 10-Q cover page. Tesla’s share count rose about 22% from mid-2025 to mid-2026, mostly restricted shares granted to its CEO, which lifts its recent value. Tech spending is BEA private investment in information processing equipment and software, plus Census private data center construction (annual rate, from 2014), as a share of GDP. The S&P 500 and CAPE are Shiller’s monthly series through September 2026. Tech share is not an “AI share”: no public series splits stock value by AI exposure. Insurance figures are from the Treasury’s Federal Insurance Office (January 2025, 2018–2022 data on 246 million policies); escrow growth is from Cotality, as reported in March 2026.
                                  • Buy now, pay later. Fed SHED, data years 2021–2025: use in the past year among all adults, and being late on a payment among users. The late-payment question has the same wording every year; the usage question’s description changed in 2023. The CFPB’s data from six lenders counted 53.6 million borrowers in 2023, which likely overcounts people, since some borrow from more than one.
